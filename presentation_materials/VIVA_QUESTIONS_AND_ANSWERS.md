# Viva Questions and Expert Technical Answers: Project 25

This document prepares the team for comprehensive technical defense and examination during the Project 25 viva. It covers 32 rigorous questions spanning multi-agent reinforcement learning theory, system architecture, empirical findings, and operational deployment.

---

### Q1. Why did you choose QMIX instead of other multi-agent algorithms?
**Answer**:  
QMIX was selected because warehouse AMR coordination requires **fully decentralized execution without real-time inter-robot communication during inference**. In warehouse environments, wireless communication channels can suffer from latency, interference, and packet loss. QMIX operates under the **Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution (CTDE)** paradigm. It guarantees that individual greedy action selection $\operatorname{argmax}_{a_i} Q_i(o_i, a_i)$ maximizes the centralized joint action-value $Q_{tot}(\mathbf{o}, \mathbf{a})$ through its monotonic mixing constraint:
$$\frac{\partial Q_{tot}}{\partial Q_i} \ge 0, \quad \forall i$$
Unlike linear value factorization methods such as VDN (Value-Decomposition Networks), QMIX uses state-conditioned hypernetworks, allowing it to represent complex, non-linear state-dependent value functions while maintaining individual action consistency.

---

### Q2. Why not use multi-agent policy gradient methods like MAPPO or MADDPG?
**Answer**:  
1. **Discrete Action Space & Sample Efficiency**: Our warehouse grid uses a compact discrete action space (4 directional moves). Deep Q-learning methods with off-policy experience replay (like QMIX) are significantly more sample-efficient than on-policy algorithms like MAPPO, which require discarding rollouts after every update.
2. **Deterministic Monotonic Deconfliction**: MADDPG uses continuous deterministic policy gradients adapted to discrete spaces via Gumbel-Softmax approximations, which introduce variance and credit assignment instability in cooperative grid-world navigation. QMIX directly estimates state-action values under an explicit credit assignment guarantee via its monotonic hypernetwork mixer.

---

### Q3. What is Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution (CTDE)?
**Answer**:  
CTDE is an architectural design pattern for multi-agent systems where:
- **During Training (Centralized)**: The learning algorithm has access to global privileged information—specifically, the centralized global state $s$ (all robot poses, cargo statuses, and full task queues) and the joint actions $\mathbf{a}$ of all agents. This information feeds the centralized mixing network to learn joint value estimates and mitigate environment non-stationarity.
- **During Execution (Decentralized)**: The centralized mixer is discarded. Each robot runs only its local agent Q-network, observing only its local 456-dimensional observation vector $o_i$ and picking actions greedily: $a_i = \operatorname{argmax}_{a} Q_i(o_i, a)$. No inter-robot communication is required at runtime.

---

### Q4. What is a mixing network in QMIX, and how does it work?
**Answer**:  
The mixing network is a feed-forward neural network that takes the individual utilities $\mathbf{q} = [Q_1(o_1, a_1), \dots, Q_N(o_N, a_N)]^T$ as inputs and outputs the scalar joint action-value $Q_{tot}$. Crucially, the weights and biases of this network are **not learned directly as static parameters**; instead, they are dynamically generated by auxiliary neural networks called **hypernetworks**, which take the centralized global warehouse state $s$ as input. The output weights are passed through an absolute value activation ($|W|$) to guarantee non-negativity.

---

### Q5. Why must the mixing network be monotonic ($\frac{\partial Q_{tot}}{\partial Q_i} \ge 0$)?
**Answer**:  
Monotonicity ensures that the Individual-Global-Max (IGM) condition holds:
$$\operatorname{argmax}_{\mathbf{a}} Q_{tot}(\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{a}) = \begin{bmatrix} \operatorname{argmax}_{a_1} Q_1(o_1, a_1) \\ \vdots \\ \operatorname{argmax}_{a_N} Q_N(o_N, a_N) \end{bmatrix}$$
If $\frac{\partial Q_{tot}}{\partial Q_i} < 0$, increasing an individual agent's utility could decrease the joint team utility, meaning an agent choosing its best local action might degrade global performance. Non-negative weights guarantee that maximizing individual utilities simultaneously maximizes the global team utility.

---

### Q6. What mathematically is a Q-value?
**Answer**:  
A Q-value, or action-value function $Q^\pi(s, a)$, represents the expected cumulative discounted return that an agent will achieve starting from state $s$, taking action $a$, and thereafter following policy $\pi$:
$$Q^\pi(s, a) = \mathbb{E}_\pi \left[ \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t R_{t+1} \;\middle|\; S_0 = s, A_0 = a \right]$$
where $\gamma \in [0, 1)$ is the discount factor ensuring convergence over infinite horizons and penalizing delayed rewards.

---

### Q7. How does $\epsilon$-greedy exploration work in multi-agent QMIX?
**Answer**:  
During training, with probability $1 - \epsilon$, agent $i$ chooses its greedy action $\operatorname{argmax}_{a} Q_i(o_i, a)$; with probability $\epsilon$, it selects a random valid action from the discrete action space to encourage state-space exploration. In our implementation, $\epsilon$ begins at $1.0$ (100% exploration) and linearly decays over 500 episodes to a minimum floor of $\epsilon_{end} = 0.05$. During evaluation and live inference, $\epsilon$ is set strictly to $0.0$ (pure greedy execution).

### Q8. What is experience replay and why is it necessary?
**Answer**:  
Experience replay stores transition tuples $(s_t, \mathbf{o}_t, \mathbf{a}_t, r_t, s_{t+1}, \mathbf{o}_{t+1}, d_t)$ in a fixed-capacity circular buffer ($5,000$ transitions). During training, minibatches of transitions are sampled uniformly at random. This breaks temporal correlations between consecutive simulation steps, stabilizes deep neural network training, and prevents catastrophic forgetting by allowing past experiences to be reused across multiple gradient updates.

---

### Q9. Why are target networks used in deep Q-learning?
**Answer**:  
In standard Q-learning, the target for the loss function is $y = r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a'; \theta)$. If the same weights $\theta$ are used to compute both the prediction $Q(s, a; \theta)$ and the target $y$, the target moves with every gradient step, causing feedback loops, oscillations, and divergence. A target network with frozen parameters $\theta^-$ computes $y = r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a'; \theta^-)$, decoupling the target from frequent parameter updates. In our system, target weights are updated via hard synchronization every 200 episodes.

### Q10. What is the multi-agent non-stationarity problem, and how does QMIX address it?
**Answer**:  
In independent learning (like IQL), each agent optimizes its own policy while treating all other agents as static features of the environment. However, because other agents are simultaneously learning and adapting their policies, the environment transition dynamics $P(s' | s, a_i)$ continuously shift from the perspective of agent $i$. This violates the Markov property. QMIX addresses non-stationarity during centralized training by conditioning the mixing network on the true global state $s$ and training the joint value function $Q_{tot}$ over the joint action vector $\mathbf{a}$.

### Q11. How is the reward function structured in this project?
**Answer**:  
The reward function balances individual progress with collective cooperative objectives:
1. **Task Delivery Reward**: $+10.0$ shared team reward when any robot completes an assigned pickup-and-delivery mission.
2. **Pickup Reward**: $+5.0$ when an agent reaches its assigned pickup coordinate.
3. **Distance Progress Shaping**: $\Delta d = d(t-1) - d(t)$, where positive progress toward the active target yields $+0.5 \times \Delta d$.
4. **Collision/Conflict Penalty**: $-2.0$ for attempted collisions or contention events.
5. **Human Corridor Penalty**: $-5.0$ for attempted intrusions into exclusion corridors.
6. **Time Step Penalty**: $-0.01$ per step to encourage minimum-makespan path execution.

### Q12. What happens when two robots attempt to move into the same cell simultaneously?
**Answer**:  
The environment executes a deterministic same-cell contention resolution:
1. The destination cell is checked against robot coordinates in the previous step.
2. **Occupant Priority**: If one of the contending robots was already occupying that cell at step $t-1$ (stationary occupant), that robot maintains priority and stays in the cell.
3. All incoming contenders targeting that occupied cell are blocked, have their actions overridden to `NO_OP`, and are added to `collision_robots`.
4. If multiple robots attempt to enter an unoccupied cell simultaneously, deterministic tie-breaking assigns the cell to the robot with the lowest ID ($\min(\text{robot\_id})$), while remaining contenders are blocked.

---

### Q13. How does the system handle head-on position swaps between two adjacent robots?
**Answer**:  
In grid worlds, a direct swap occurs when Robot A at cell $X$ attempts to move to cell $Y$, while Robot B at cell $Y$ simultaneously attempts to move to cell $X$. Physical AMRs cannot pass through each other in a narrow single-lane aisle. The environment explicitly checks for direct pairwise swaps:
$$\text{If } proposed[A] == previous[B] \text{ and } proposed[B] == previous[A],$$
both robots are marked as conflicting, both moves are blocked to `NO_OP`, and both remain at their current coordinates.

---

### Q14. What is the precise distinction between a safety conflict and a physical collision?
**Answer**:  
- **Physical Collision**: An event where two robot meshes actually occupy the same $(x, y)$ grid coordinate in committed environment state. In our evaluated system, physical collisions were **zero (0.000)** because the simulator's pre-action contention resolution blocks conflicting moves before positions update.
- **Safety Conflict**: An intervention event where a robot proposed an action that *would* have caused a collision, swap, or boundary violation, but was intercepted and overridden by the safety gate. At $N=10$, QMIX produced only 2.15 safety conflict events per episode versus 88.85 for IQL (a 97.6% reduction).

---

### Q15. How does the pre-action safety layer enforce human-worker corridor safety?
**Answer**:  
The `SafetyLayer` operates as a deterministic, non-bypassable pre-action gate. Before any proposed action modifies the environment state, the safety gate computes the destination cell $c_{target} = c_{current} + \Delta a$. If $c_{target}$ falls within the annotated set of human corridors $\mathcal{C}_{human}$, the action is instantly replaced with `NO_OP` (`0` movement) and a safety override event is logged. This layer is hard-coded in the simulation loop and cannot be bypassed or weakened by policy weights or negative rewards.

### Q16. How are warehouse tasks generated and assigned?
**Answer**:  
Tasks are generated by sampling pickup-and-delivery coordinate pairs from opposite halves of the walkable warehouse floor to ensure realistic travel distances across aisles. Endpoints are constrained to be non-corridor and non-obstacle cells. In the benchmark and training rollouts, each robot is initialized with one active task queue. When an agent reaches the pickup point, its `carrying_cargo` flag sets to `True`; when it reaches the delivery depot with cargo, the task completes, and a new mission is assigned.

### Q17. How does the model scale from 5 to 10 to 20 robots?
**Answer**:  
- **At $N=5$**: Low traffic density; both QMIX and IQL achieve 78% completion, but QMIX reduces conflict interventions by 31x.
- **At $N=10$**: Moderate density; QMIX demonstrates clear coordination superiority, completing 37% versus 23% for IQL (+14 percentage points) with a 97.6% conflict reduction.
- **At $N=20$**: Severe traffic saturation; both learned policies collapse (<1% completion) due to 2-cell oscillation in congested two-meter aisles.

### Q18. What was the exact root cause of the N=20 policy oscillation problem?
**Answer**:  
Trajectory trace analysis revealed that in dense two-way traffic, two opposing robots meeting in a narrow aisle both attempt to move forward, get blocked by conflict resolution, and then learn that stepping backward and forward again incurs only minimal step penalties while keeping them near their goal. Because distance-based reward shaping rewards reducing distance and cancels out over cyclic paths without directional memory, the greedy policy becomes trapped in a localized two-cell oscillation loop: alternating between $(x, y)$ and $(x+1, y)$ indefinitely. Breaking this requires macro-actions, temporal history (e.g. GRU/RNN cells), or spatial one-way traffic routing.

### Q19. How was checkpoint selection performed, and how did you prevent test leakage?
**Answer**:  
We used a strict three-tier protocol:
1. Training ran on base seeds 42, 43, and 44, saving snapshots every 100 episodes.
2. Intermediate snapshots were evaluated across 20 **validation task queues** (separate seeds) to select the best checkpoint: Snapshot 600 for $N=5$, Snapshot 1000 for $N=10$, and Snapshot 200 for $N=20$.
3. Final evaluation was performed on a completely independent **frozen held-out benchmark** (seed 816597421, manifest hash `ddc457ee...`). The held-out scenarios were never used during training or checkpoint selection.

### Q20. Why did you use bootstrap confidence intervals and paired statistical tests?
**Answer**:  
Reinforcement learning evaluation across a finite number of episodes (e.g. 20 episodes) produces high variance due to stochastic task spawn locations and initial robot configurations. A simple point estimate (like 78%) does not communicate estimation uncertainty. We computed **95% bootstrap confidence intervals with 10,000 resamples** for all metrics. Furthermore, because algorithms were evaluated on matched scenario seeds, we conducted paired differences tests with **Holm-Bonferroni correction** to control the family-wise error rate across multiple hypotheses.

---

### Q21. Why is this project NOT a classification or regression problem?
**Answer**:  
Classification and regression assume independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) data samples with static ground-truth target labels $y \in \mathcal{Y}$. In warehouse robot coordination:
1. There are no static labels; the system must discover optimal behaviors through trial and error.
2. Actions have delayed, long-term consequences that affect future states (sequential decision making).
3. The environment state distribution changes dynamically as a direct consequence of the agents' own learned actions (violating the i.i.d. assumption).

---

### Q22. Why are accuracy, precision, recall, and Brier score inappropriate evaluation metrics here?
**Answer**:  
Accuracy and F1-score evaluate class prediction correctness against fixed ground truth. In our system, an action is not 'correct' or 'incorrect' in isolation; its quality depends on future states and other agents' moves. Brier score measures probability calibration for categorical predictions; however, QMIX outputs expected cumulative discounted returns $Q(s, a) \in \mathbb{R}$, which are unbounded utilities, not probabilities. Evaluating Brier score or ROC-AUC on Q-values is mathematically unsound.

### Q23. What is the P6 checkpoint compatibility standard implemented in this project?
**Answer**:  
The P6 standard ensures that QMIX checkpoints cannot be loaded into incompatible architectures. In multi-agent QMIX, while the individual agent network is shared across robots, the **mixing network dimensions scale directly with fleet size $N$** ($s \in \mathbb{R}^{11 \times N}$, hypernetworks generating $W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 32}$). P6 introduces Schema V2 metadata validation, which verifies tensor names, layer dimensions, and fleet size $N$ before loading weights. If an $N=5$ checkpoint is loaded into an $N=10$ controller, P6 raises an explicit descriptive exception rather than silently initializing random weights or skipping parameters.

### Q24. How does the React/Three.js frontend communicate with the Python backend?
**Answer**:  
The frontend connects to the FastAPI backend via a bi-directional WebSocket connection at `ws://127.0.0.1:8000/ws`. At 10 Hz (every 100 milliseconds), the backend broadcasts the full environment state as a structured JSON payload containing robot positions, cargo statuses, recent collisions, safety override flags, and KPI metrics. The React client parses this telemetry, and Three.js smoothly interpolates AMR mesh positions and rotations using GSAP tweens. The client also sends control commands (Start, Pause, Reset, Fleet Size change) back to the server over the same WebSocket.

### Q25. What role does ONNX play in this system?
**Answer**:  
ONNX (Open Neural Network Exchange) provides a framework-independent representation of our trained PyTorch `AgentQNetwork`. Using `src/marl/export_onnx.py`, we exported the trained network and verified numerical output parity against PyTorch using ONNX Runtime. The maximum absolute difference between PyTorch and ONNX output tensors was **$2.39 \times 10^{-7}$**, well below the $10^{-5}$ tolerance threshold. This enables high-throughput, low-latency C++ or edge runtime execution in our `/decide` REST inference service.

### Q28. What are the key remaining technical limitations of this project?
**Answer**:  
1. **N=20 Congestion Saturation**: Both learned policies collapsed at $N=20$ due to cyclic 2-cell oscillation in narrow aisles.
2. **Simplified Kinematics**: The primary simulation operates on a discrete grid with 4 orthogonal actions rather than continuous non-holonomic AMR kinematics (e.g., differential drive with acceleration limits).
3. **Static Task Allocation**: Tasks are assigned at spawn time rather than through dynamic market-based or auction-based re-allocation during execution.
4. **NFR-1 Target**: QMIX achieved 78.0% completion at $N=5$, narrowly missing the 80.0% acceptance threshold by 2 deliveries.

### Q29. What would be required to deploy this system on real physical warehouse robots?
**Answer**:  
1. **Continuous Kinematics & Low-Level Control**: Replacing discrete grid stepping with a low-level motion controller (e.g. Model Predictive Control or ROS 2 Nav2) executing velocity commands $(v, \omega)$.
2. **SLAM & Sensor Fusion**: Real-time localization using onboard 2D/3D LiDAR, wheel odometry, and visual fiducial markers (AprilTags).
3. **Asynchronous Execution & Latency**: Handling variable sensor delays and asynchronous execution cycles instead of lock-step simulation ticks.
4. **Certified Safety Hardware**: Integrating physical safety LiDAR bumpers and ISO 3691-4 certified emergency stop hardware independent of software policies.

### Q30. Why did QMIX achieve 78% completion at N=5 instead of the required 80%?
**Answer**:  
Across the 20 held-out evaluation episodes, QMIX completed 78 out of 100 assigned deliveries (78.0%), with a 95% bootstrap confidence interval of [70.0%, 86.0%]. The target was missed by exactly 2 deliveries. The primary reason was that 4 episodes encountered temporary aisle contention near delivery depots within the 100-step horizon limit. While the 80% target falls within the 95% confidence interval, we report this result strictly as missed rather than claiming a pass.
